## Scene 1 — The Two Crowns
**A.V. 2325, Ilreth. Dramatizes:** RAV-A0-V6-007, copybook marginalia — *"The real King came to see me. He asked if the songs were breaking. Kings shouldn't ask children that. I told him the truth, which is that the song isn't breaking — it's being listened to. He got older in front of me. I'm sorry I did that."*

---

They gave him the good chair and it was too low.

That was the first thing Osric III of House Danterre noticed about the room where they were keeping the child: someone had carried in a chair from the Lesser Audience and set it by the window, and the seat was four inches lower than his knees now liked. Thirty-eight years of being handed the good chair, and nobody had thought to mention to the furniture that he was seventy-one.

He sat anyway. Slowly. He had gotten good at slowly.

The child was at the table with a bowl of winter apples and a sheet of paper ruled into columns, and she was writing in it with the concentration of a clerk finishing a shift. She did not stand when he came in. Nobody had told her to, or somebody had and it had not taken.

"That's a tally," he said.

"It's the score." She did not look up. "They play in the court. If I keep it, it stays fair."

"You don't play?"

"I did once." She wrote something small in the last column. "It went strange."

Outside and four floors down, someone shouted about a foul, and someone else shouted that it was not, and a third voice — older, tired, a groundskeeper's voice — told them both to shift the marker back where it belonged. The ordinary racket of a Tuesday. Osric found he wanted to sit and listen to it for a while, which was not why he had come, and was very nearly why he had come.

"They tell me your name is Echo."

"It's the name that was left." She capped her pen. Looked up. Her eyes were an entirely unremarkable brown. "Would you like an apple? Denner brings them. He thinks I don't know he's a guard."

"Does he."

"He pretends they're from nobody." She pushed the bowl an inch toward him, which was as far as her arm went. "I write 'dear nobody, thank you' in my book. Somebody should."

Osric took an apple because it was easier than explaining that he could not eat one, and turned it over in his hands, and did not eat it. The skin was cold. Winter fruit from the northern glasshouses; three chimes apiece in the Ilreth markets and the Recorder ran a column of complaint about the price every autumn like a liturgy.

He had rehearsed this. In the carriage he had rehearsed it four separate ways, and every one of them had been a way of not asking, and he was too old and too tired and the chair was too low.

"Are the songs breaking?"

The child went still. Not frightened. Attentive — the way Yareen went still, he thought, and hated himself for the comparison, because Yareen was eleven hundred years old and this was a girl with apple on her fingers.

"You're not supposed to ask me that," she said.

"No."

"Kings shouldn't ask children that."

"No," he agreed. "They shouldn't."

She thought about it. He watched her think, and he understood with a clarity that arrived like cold water that she was not deciding whether to tell him. She was deciding how much of it he could carry.

"The song isn't breaking," she said at last. "It's being listened to."

The room did not change. The shouting in the court went on. Somewhere below, a bell rang the half hour and the Glassline rails hummed their civic pitch through the stone of the floor as they had hummed every hour of his reign, and the winter apple sat in his hands, and Osric felt the whole architecture of his life quietly rearrange itself into a smaller shape.

*Listened to.* Not failing. Not wearing out. Not — as he had let himself hope, on the worst nights, in the coward's part of himself — simply the ordinary thinning of an old thing, which would mean the fault was in the world and not in the man holding it.

Attended. By something.

"Ah," he said.

He had presided over the harmonic renewal thirty-eight times. He had felt the rites run long, year over year, the cantors adding voices to reach a pitch that used to come with fewer. He had asked them, twice, whether that meant anything, and been told, twice, in the beautiful evasive grammar of the Radiant Hymn, that the Crown's own faith was the surest instrument. He had let himself be told.

"How long," he said, and stopped, because he did not know how to finish it in a way that was not a child's question.

Echo picked up the pen again. Put it down.

"You got older," she said. "Just now. While I was talking." And then, with real distress, the first crack in her, "I'm sorry. I didn't mean to do that."

"It isn't yours to be sorry for."

"It might be."

Osric laughed. It surprised them both. It was not a good laugh — it was thin and it went on a half-beat too long, the laugh of a man setting something heavy down on a table that might not hold it — but it was real, and the child watched it with frank scientific interest, and did not join in, and did not look away either.

"May I ask you something," he said, "that no one will write down?"

"Denner writes everything down."

"Then let him."

She shrugged, which he took for permission.

"If the Verse answers you now," Osric said, "what am I?"

He had not known he was going to say it. It came out of him in the plain flat voice of a man reading a ledger entry aloud, and it sat in the room, and he would have given a province to have it back.

Echo looked at him for a long moment.

"I don't know," she said. "I thought you were the King."

She did not want his chair. That would have been easier.

There was law for usurpers. There was none for this.

He had wanted, he realized, for her to want it. A usurper he knew what to do with. A usurper was a problem with a procedure — and in all the centuries of the Verse the Mark had never once returned to a King still alive to see it.

"I am going to tell you a thing about being King," Osric said, "and then I am going to go, because my knees have opinions about that chair. Are you listening?"

"Yes."

"For thirty-eight years, every difficult morning, I have told myself: it does not matter what I want, it matters what the office requires." He set the apple back in the bowl, carefully, so it would not roll. "That is a very good way to live. It is also a very good place to hide. I have hidden there so long that when someone finally asks me what I am, I find I have not kept an answer."

Echo considered this with the gravity she gave the rondel scores.

"That's why you asked me," she said. "Not because I'd know."

"No," he said. "Because you'd say."

He got up. It took a moment, and the child did not offer to help, which he found he was grateful for.

At the door he stopped. "The score," he said. "Who's winning?"

"Everyone, a little." She had the pen back in her hand. "I keep it very fairly."

"Yes," said the King of the Crown, whose office had just been called into question by the fabric of reality itself, and who had thirty-one appointments before supper and no idea what he was. "I imagine you do."

Behind him, four floors down, the argument about the marker started up again, and someone's mother shouted from a window that if the game did not quiet down there would be no game, and the game did not quiet down, and there was still a game.

## Scene 2 — The Halloway Contract
**A.V. 2339, Eclipsera. Dramatizes:** the founding-year argument between Saint Felia and Tobbin Fenn that produced the Open Rose's first standing rule.

---

The Open Rose's first chapter house was a coaching inn the order could not yet afford, and the assembly room still smelled of horses.

They had scrubbed it twice. It had not helped. Rain came down the chimney and made a sound in the grate like someone shifting their weight, and eleven people sat around a table meant for eight, drinking out of whatever had been in the kitchen. Tobbin Fenn had the dented tin mug, because he had arrived last and because he always took the dented tin mug, which Ser Alda Brenner had begun to suspect was a bit.

The factor from the Sennet League had brought his own chair.

"Two thousand chimes on acceptance," he said. "Six on completion. The League will indemnify the order against any Crown inquiry, and I am authorized to make that in writing tonight." He had a good voice. He had clearly used it in better rooms than this one. "Governor Denning Halloway of Pellhurst has held the river tariff for nine years. The province cannot afford him. We would like him removed from office by whatever means the Rose finds appropriate, and we do not intend to ask which."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The rain shifted its weight in the grate.

"You should hear Anneke," Felia said.

The woman from Pellhurst had been sitting at the end of the table for an hour with her hands around a cup she had not drunk from. Anneke Roud had travelled eleven days to be in this room. She was a miller's widow, and she had the specific tiredness of a person who has already told her story to four institutions that listened politely.

She told it again. The tariff. Then the second tariff, on the grain that moved to pay the first. Then the winter the mill went, and the winter after that, when the Governor's men took the Ferris family's boy for arrears and the boy came back with a hand that would not close. She named eleven people. She had them in order, which meant she had practiced.

When she finished she said, "I am not asking you to kill anybody. I want to be clear that I am not asking that. I am asking whether anyone is going to do anything at all," and then she drank the cold cup because her hands needed something to do.

Felia let the silence sit a moment before she filled it. She had learned to do that; she had not always known how.

"We can do this," she said. "Cleanly. He travels between the residence and the tariff house four days in seven and his guard is twelve men who have never been shot at. It doesn't have to be a killing. He can be taken, tried in the Moot's provincial court, and replaced by the deputy, who is not good but is not this." She looked down the table. "I've read the ledgers Anneke brought. He is going to take another sixty families this year. That's not a projection, that's his own arithmetic. He wrote it down."

Ser Corwyn Dace said the Rose had the strength for it. Ser Alda said she wanted the indemnity in writing before anyone agreed to anything. Somebody asked about the deputy's cousin in the customs house. The room was moving.

"I think we shouldn't take it," Tobbin said.

He said it badly. He said it into his mug, and half of them didn't hear, and he had to say it again with his face up, and by then it was worse because everyone was looking. A Levy clerk in a room full of knights, disagreeing with a living saint.

"Go on," said Felia.

"It's — " He stopped. Started again. "It's not the Governor. I'd take the Governor. If Anneke's neighbour walked in here with a hammer and asked to borrow a horse, I'd saddle it." Anneke did not smile, but something moved in her face. "It's the coin. It's the League's coin. If we take money to decide which government deserves to stand, then—"

"Then what?"

"Then every city too weak to stop us lives by our permission." It came out whole, and it surprised him, and he put the mug down. "That's what we'd be. Not knights. A government nobody elected, funded by whoever can pay us. And we would be a *good* one, that's the part that frightens me, we'd be good at it for years. That's how it would happen. Not because we went bad. Because we were competent, and cheap, and correct."

"The League is paying for a tariff to end," said the factor, mildly. "Not for a throne."

"You're paying for a man to be removed from an office by people with no authority to remove him," Tobbin said. "I don't know what else to call that."

Felia set her hands flat on the table.

"Tobbin. The people dying in Pellhurst this winter do not care what precedent saves us in ten years." She said it evenly, which was worse than if she had said it hot. "I know the argument. I have made the argument. I made it for the Veil for nineteen years, and the argument was always *the institution must be protected*, and the institution was always protected, and I was the thing they protected it with." She let that sit. "I did not leave in order to become careful."

"I know."

"Then say the rest of it."

And he did, because she had asked, and because he had never once in his life been able to leave a true thing unsaid to her face.

"The rest of it is that you're the one saying yes," Tobbin said. "That's the whole problem, and I don't know how to say it politely, so I'm going to say it badly. Nobody here can tell you no. The Crown wants you obedient, the Church wants you holy, the Veil wants you theirs, and everyone at this table wants you *right*, and if a saint says a governor should fall, then he falls, and next year it'll be a magistrate, and the year after that it'll be someone who only looked like Halloway from the outside." He was red to the ears. "You didn't leave the Veil to be careful. Fine. But you didn't leave it to be the Word of the King with better manners, either."

The rain went on in the grate.

Ser Alda Brenner examined the table's grain with great attention. The factor from the Sennet League, to his considerable credit, had stopped writing.

Felia did not answer for a long moment. Under everything, always now, the names hummed — the counted dead she had carried out of the Undersong, a low continuous sound that no room had been quiet enough to hide since. She had stopped expecting it to stop. She listened to it and to the rain and to a saddle horse being led through the yard below, and thought about sixty families and a boy's hand that would not close.

"Anneke," she said. "How long did it take you to get here?"

"Eleven days."

"Then one more will not kill anybody, and if I'm wrong about that I will carry it." Felia looked around the table. "We decide in the morning. Nobody signs tonight. And I want it in the book, from today: this order takes no contract on the day it's offered. Every one of them sleeps a night. Anyone in the room may speak against it — anyone, at any rank, and it costs them nothing."

"That's not a decision," said Ser Corwyn.

"No," she agreed. "It's a delay. I'm told they're different."

They took it up again the next morning, and again the morning after that, and the Rose declined the Sennet League's coin.

---

Afterward, in the yard, in the rain, Tobbin was working a stone out of a horse's hoof because someone had to and the stablehand had gone to bed.

"You were very brave in there," Felia said, "and I am not certain I have forgiven you."

"That's fair."

"Sixty families."

"I know." He did not look up from the hoof. "I'm not going to tell you the ledger comes out my way. I don't think it does."

"Then why?"

Tobbin thought about it for longer than she expected. The horse leaned on him, and he leaned back, and eventually he set the hoof down and straightened up with both hands on his back like a man twice his age.

"Because I can't see ten years out," he said. "You can't either. Nobody in that room can. All I know is what we'd be on the way there." He wiped his hands on his coat, which did nothing. "Have you eaten?"

"What?"

"It's nearly two. Have you eaten anything today."

"No."

"Right," said Tobbin Fenn, and went to see what the kitchen had, while the rain came down on the first chapter house of the Order of the Open Rose and the names went on humming under everything, and in Pellhurst Province, four hundred miles north, Denning Halloway held his office for two more years.

---

## Scene 3 — The Quiet Tax
**A.V. 2324, Eclipsera — Arclight Sprawl.** *Complete short story rather than an excerpted scene. Division 13 incident record, later sealed under Mirror Sector municipal-noise remediation archives.*

---

By the time someone reported the silence, it had already eaten three blocks.

Not complete silence.

Complete silence would have frightened people.

This was gentler.

The Glassline still whispered through its rails overhead. Rain still clicked against the illuminated awnings of Bracken Street. Somewhere above the underpass, advertisements sang about winter coats, cheap rune insurance, and a new Perch Coffee two stations east.

But beneath the causeway, things had become soft.

Footsteps landed without weight.

Car doors shut politely.

Arguments ended before anyone remembered why they were angry.

And seventeen people living beneath Transit Pillar 91 had stopped speaking altogether.

Constabulary Patrolman Edrin Vale wrote the first report at 03:17.

POSSIBLE CIVIC HARMONIC MALFUNCTION.

At 03:22, the report passed through the municipal resonance network.

At 03:22 and six seconds, a Raven signature buried inside that network heard something wrong.

At 03:41, the report ceased to exist.

At 04:06, Division 13 arrived wearing orange coats marked:

ARCLIGHT TRANSIT AUTHORITY — ACOUSTIC SAFETY.

Agent Corren Vey disliked the coats.

"They make us look like road workers."

"We are road workers," said Mara Sen.

She was Queen's Garden, medical bureau, carrying a black case disguised as a municipal testing kit. She stepped over a sleeping man without waking him.

Corren glanced at her.

"We are carrying rifles."

"Road workers in Eclipsera."

"Fair."

The third member of the team, Raven Listener Aven Draal, said nothing.

He had been silent since they left Mirror Sector Command seven levels beneath the transit grid.

That was beginning to bother Corren.

"Draal."

The Listener raised one finger.

Wait.

So they waited.

Rainwater ran through the gutters.

A tram passed overhead.

Its harmonic engine should have produced the familiar low C that every Eclipsera child knew before they learned the national anthem.

Corren felt the vibration through his boots.

He heard nothing.

Draal opened his eyes.

"Twenty-eight percent attenuation."

Mara frowned. "At the street?"

"Thirty-one now."

"Growing?"

Draal nodded.

Corren's joking stopped.

He looked beneath the causeway.

There were nine of them.

Men and women sitting against concrete pillars with cups before their feet. Blankets. Handwritten signs. A veteran with one mechanical hand. An old elf woman wearing three coats. A young human man asleep against a shopping trolley.

People passed them without looking.

A commuter dropped a silver ring into one of the cups.

The coin struck the metal bottom.

No sound came.

The commuter hesitated.

Then continued walking.

Corren's hand moved toward the compact I&S rifle beneath his coat.

Mara caught his wrist.

"No."

"I know doctrine."

"Then follow it."

Most of these people, the Raven Register warned, would be exactly what they appeared to be.

Poor.

Sick.

Lost.

Unlucky.

The Hushfolk did not create Eclipsera's forgotten people.

They simply understood the value of living among them.

Identify the signature. Sever the conductor. Leave the street its innocents.

Corren released the rifle.

"Fine."

Mara opened her case.

Inside were sandwiches.

Corren stared at them.

"You brought sandwiches."

"I brought forty."

"This is an operation."

"Yes."

She began handing them out.

Some people took them immediately. Some suspiciously. One man asked whether they were from the Church.

"Transit Authority," Mara said.

The man looked at the sandwich.

"Transit Authority gives out ham now?"

"New program."

"Government's gone mad."

"Completely."

He took two.

Corren watched Mara move down the row.

The Hushfolk did not react.

That was the first problem.

The second was the cups.

Draal crouched beside one.

Copper bits. Two silver rings. A button.

And beneath them, scratched into the tin, a rune.

Not Crown standard.

Not Veil.

The geometry curled inward.

Inverse.

Draal did not touch it.

"Found the drain."

Corren's jaw tightened.

"Which one?"

"Not a person."

"What?"

Draal pointed.

"All of them."

Corren looked along the underpass.

Nine cups.

Nine runes.

Nine tiny mouths drinking from the same street.

"Where's it going?"

Draal tilted his head.

Listening.

Then turned toward the pedestrian tunnel.

At its far end sat an old man.

Corren could have sworn he had not been there a moment before.

Maybe he had.

That was the trouble with Eclipsera.

There were people a city could look directly at and still refuse to see.

The man sat beneath a dead advertising panel wearing a grey coat several decades out of fashion. No cup rested before him.

His hands lay open on his knees.

His lips were sewn shut.

Not metaphorically.

Black thread passed through flesh.

Mara stopped distributing food.

"Almoner."

The old man's eyes opened.

Every light beneath the causeway went dark.

Corren drew his rifle.

The sound vanished from the world.

Not muffled.

Gone.

His weapon came free without the scrape of fabric.

Mara shouted something.

Nothing reached him.

Then the Quietors stood.

Three figures detached themselves from the tunnel walls.

Corren had seen photographs.

Photographs had been kinder.

They wore ordinary clothes. Work boots. Rain jackets. One had a Crown Transit employee badge clipped to his chest.

None carried guns.

They did not need them.

The first crossed ten meters before Corren understood it had moved.

A fist struck his vest.

No impact.

No grunt.

Only the sudden knowledge of pain.

Corren fired twice.

The rifle bucked against his shoulder in perfect silence.

One round missed.

The other caught the Quietor high in the chest.

It staggered.

Mara was already moving toward the Hushfolk row.

Not away.

Toward.

She kicked the first cup over.

Coins scattered.

The old elf woman jerked as if waking from deep water.

Mara kicked another.

Then another.

The Almoner's eyes followed her.

Corren understood.

The cups were not merely collecting sound.

They were anchoring him.

He fired at the second Quietor.

Draal reached the fourth cup and slammed his runic gauntlet against the concrete.

Blue Veil script flashed once.

Then failed.

The silence swallowed the spell.

Null-adjacent interference.

Of course.

Draal looked annoyed.

Then he picked up a brick.

The Raven Listener smashed the cup with it.

Corren would remember that afterward.

Thousands of years of accumulated harmonic doctrine.

Secret sciences.

Neural-keyed runecraft.

A distributed intelligence network hidden inside the infrastructure of the most advanced city on the continent.

And when the Old Gods reached through the gutter to strangle Eclipsera—

sometimes the correct answer was a brick.

Mara overturned the sixth cup.

Corren shot the third Quietor through the knee.

Draal broke the seventh.

A homeless man saw what they were doing, looked at his own cup, and hurled it into the street.

Eight.

The Almoner stood.

He was very tall.

Nobody had noticed that while he was sitting.

Corren aimed.

The Almoner opened his sewn mouth.

The thread snapped.

Nothing came out.

But the world leaned toward him.

Corren felt something inside his chest being pulled forward.

His breath.

His heartbeat.

The tiny involuntary noises of blood and bone and living tissue.

Not sound.

The possibility of sound.

Mara was suddenly beside him.

She held the ninth cup.

She had not destroyed it.

The veteran with the mechanical hand was gripping her sleeve.

His mouth moved.

Corren could not hear him.

Mara could not either.

But she understood.

The cup belonged to him.

His possessions were inside.

A photograph.

Two coins.

A little wooden bird.

Mara emptied them gently into his hand.

Then she crushed the cup beneath her boot.

Sound returned like an explosion.

Rain.

Traffic.

The Glassline screaming overhead.

Someone crying.

Corren's rifle firing.

The shot struck the Almoner through the sternum.

For one impossible second the old man remained standing.

Then Draal spoke.

Not loudly.

One tone.

Pure.

Correct.

The Almoner folded around it.

His body collapsed inward as though something inside him had finally remembered that bones were supposed to occupy space.

Then he was only an old man in an obsolete grey coat.

Dead beneath an advertising screen.

The rain continued.

A tram passed overhead.

Low C.

Perfectly ordinary.

For several seconds nobody moved.

Then the veteran with the mechanical hand said:

"My bird."

Mara looked down.

The little wooden bird had fallen into a puddle.

She picked it up, dried it carefully on the orange sleeve of her municipal coat, and handed it back.

"Sorry."

The veteran turned it over.

"You people really Transit Authority?"

Corren looked at the dead Quietors.

The rifle in his hands.

The shattered cups.

The corpse with black thread hanging from its lips.

"No," he said.

Mara looked at him.

Corren sighed.

Then amended:

"Subcontractors."

The veteran considered this.

"That makes more sense."

At 09:00, the official report blamed a resonance regulator beneath the Glassline.

At 11:30, Crown engineers replaced a regulator that had never been broken.

By afternoon, three news channels were arguing about neglected transit infrastructure.

By evening, the constabulary had forgotten Patrolman Vale's original report because the report had never existed.

Mirror Sector marked the incident:

CONTAINED — MHOR SIGNATURE PROBABLE.

Three Quietors dead.

One Almoner terminated.

Nine civilians medically cleared.

Zero public disclosure.

The Queen's Garden spent sixty-three chimes from an account meant for "storm-water displacement mitigation" putting everyone beneath Transit Pillar 91 in temporary rooms.

The Raven objected to the accounting code.

The Garden ignored them.

Two days later, commuters passed beneath the causeway again.

They talked too loudly.

Shoes struck pavement.

Coins rang against cups.

A child screamed because his mother would not buy him a sugar bun.

Someone played a cheap guitar badly outside the station entrance.

The city had its noise back.

No one knew it had nearly lost it.

Which, according to the Veil, meant the operation had been a success.
